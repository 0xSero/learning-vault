# The Adversary Method

> [!abstract] TL;DR
> The working pattern of the session: **you propose a reading → the agent attacks it (assumption by assumption) → you refine.** It's how the [[The Sacrifice Thread]] went from four lines to five, and how the "I am" claims stayed attached to the data instead of floating into a doctrine. The method works because a proposal is testable against a corpus; it fails in three predictable ways (sycophancy, over-correction, fabricated referents), and this note names each with the session's own examples.

## The pattern

1. **Propose.** A reading in your own words — even rough. ("God desires sacrifice in return. Jesus is god because he understood the offer.")
2. **Attack.** The adversary goes assumption by assumption:
   - *What does this assume?* (That God needs feeding. That the mountain's purpose was pedagogical. That understanding is a sufficient condition for divinity.)
   - *What would have to be true for this to hold?* (That the text presents sacrifice as repayment. That Gen 22 states its purpose as teaching. That the prophets didn't also understand the plan.)
   - *Does the text say that?* (Isa 1:11, Amos 5:25, Mic 6:8 — no. Gen 22:2, 12 — the stated purpose is a test. Isaiah 53 — the servant knows, and isn't God.)
3. **Refine.** Keep what survived; restate what didn't, in the form the data actually supports. The result of the session: three attacks, one corrected line each, and a five-line version that is load-bearing.

## Why it works

- **A proposal is testable against a corpus.** The God research turned the Bible into a checkable dataset. "Does the text say X?" stops being a feeling and becomes a search.
- **The adversary catches the load-bearing errors, not the surface ones.** The surface of the sacrifice reading was fine (the skeleton is real); the load was in the economics, the stated-purpose claim, and the direction of the proof. Surface edits would have polished the error.
- **It protects against the two classic AI failure modes:** the yes-machine (confirming what you already believe) and the fabricator (producing confident content with no referent). An adversary that actually attacks is the first; refusing to answer the referent-less question is the second.

## The three failure modes (and the session's examples)

> [!warning] 1. Sycophancy — the yes-machine
> The failure: the agent confirms the proposal, maybe with a compliment, and the error survives with better formatting.
> The tell: no assumption is ever named; the response uses "yes, and" more than "here's what breaks."
> The session's guard: the attacks were *named* — "the economics are backwards," "the mountain's stated purpose," "the inverted proof" — each with the verses that do the killing.

> [!warning] 2. Over-correction
> The failure: the adversary rejects the whole proposal when only the load is bad, throwing out a real skeleton with the error.
> The tell: the correction discards parts of the proposal that the data actually supports.
> The session's guard: line 4 ("self sacrifice to feed god's creation") was *kept* — the direction was right. Only the "return" in line 1 was removed. The refinement preserved the skeleton and swapped the three weak loads.

> [!warning] 3. Fabricated referents
> The failure: a question with no object gets a confident answer anyway.
> The tell: the answer names a "fixed symbol" or a "tradition" that, if you followed the link, wouldn't be there.
> The session's example: [[The 23-Second Probe]] — the correct answer was a decomposition plus a question back, not a symbol.

## The one rule underneath all three

> [!important]
> A count proves frequency, not meaning. A correction must be *load-bearing* (it names the specific assumption and the specific text that breaks it). A refusal must be *referential* (it says what's missing, not just "I can't answer"). Anything less is styling, not thinking.

## How to run it (prompt shape)

The shape that worked in the session, reusable:

> "Here's my reading: [four lines]. Stress-test it against the text: name each assumption, find what breaks it, and give me the sharpened version. Keep what survives."

That single prompt produced the whole [[The Sacrifice Thread]] note.
