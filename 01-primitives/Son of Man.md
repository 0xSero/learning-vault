# Son of Man

> [!abstract] TL;DR
> "Son of man" (Aramaic *bar enash*) is almost a plain word in Jesus's own language — "the human one" — but he picks it as his title because of Daniel 7, where that phrase points to a heavenly figure who receives the eternal kingdom. The title pulls in two directions at once: *fully man* and *the one given the kingdom*. It is used **only by Jesus of himself** (~69 times in the Gospels), while "Son of God" is used only *about* him by others — the two titles work in opposite directions, which is why scholars treat the son-of-man sayings as his most authentic words.

## The original words

| Language | Form | Plain sense |
|---|---|---|
| Aramaic (Jesus's speech) | *bar enash* | "son of a human" — in everyday Aramaic just "a man" |
| Hebrew | *ben ʾāḏām* | same literal sense; in Ezekiel it is God's form of address to a *mere human* (90+ times) |
| Greek (the Gospels) | *ho huios tou anthropou* | "the son of man/humanity" — the article makes it a title |

The baseline is humility: "son of man" is what you call an unremarkable human. That is exactly why it can do the thing it does.

## Where the weight comes from: Daniel 7

Daniel 7:13 (Aramaic *kebar enash*, "one like a son of man"): a figure comes **on the clouds of heaven** to the Ancient of Days and receives "dominion, glory and a kingdom" that all nations serve, everlasting. The LXX renders it "one like a son of a man."

When Jesus says "you shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:62), he quotes Daniel 7:13 *and* Psalm 110:1 in one breath and applies the throne-figure to himself. That is the decisive claim inside an otherwise modest-sounding phrase.

## The three usage clusters

1. **Humble/earthly** — "the Son of man hath not where to lay his head" (Matt 8:20); "the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister" (Mark 10:45). Here it simply means "I, as a man."
2. **Authoritative** — "the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10); "the Son of man is Lord of the sabbath" (Mark 2:28); "the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).
3. **Exalted/apocalyptic** — the coming-on-clouds sayings (Mark 13:26; Matt 24:30; Matt 26:64); the passion predictions ("the Son of man is delivered into the hands of men"); John 3:13 — "the Son of man which **is in heaven**"; John 5:27 — "hath given him authority to execute judgment, because he is the Son of man."

The range *is* the meaning: he is the true human (son of Adam) who is also the Daniel 7 figure to whom the kingdom was given.

## Across translations

| Version | Rendering | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "the Son of man" (lowercase m) | closest to the literal; can read as banal |
| RSV / NASB / ESV / NIV / NLT | "the Son of Man" | capitalized as a fixed title; signals the Daniel 7 weight |
| Literal/academic | "the human one" / "the Man" | strips the title feel to show the plain Aramaic |
| Targum (Aramaic) Ps 8:5 | "what is the *son of man* (bar enash)" | the old Aramaic translation of "what is man" — the link some scholars trace from Psalm 8 to Daniel 7 to Jesus |
| New World Translation (JW) | "the Man at Hand" | flattens it to a time-reference — the clearest example of a translation choosing a meaning |

The translation loss common to all English versions: "a man" (*ton anthrōpon*) has no title in the Greek, yet English reads it as one.

## Scholarly notes

- The title is **only ever his self-designation** in the NT (~69 times, all in his mouth; a few Acts references are *about* him, e.g. Acts 7:56).
- "Son of God" is **only ever said about him** by others (the demon in Mark 1:24, the transfiguration voice, the centurion, Thomas). The asymmetry is structural.
- Because no one else in the early church used "son of man" as a title for Jesus, it is unlikely to be a later church invention — the sayings are considered the most authentic layer of the Gospels.
- The debate: does the title mean "the man" (generic humanity, a modest self-reference) or "the Daniel figure" (a messianic claim)? The consensus is that Jesus held both levels at once — which is the point.
